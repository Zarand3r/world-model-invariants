\section{Enforcing the recovered law improves the model's own predictions}
\label{sec:intervention}

If $C$ is the quantity the model's dynamics actually respect, then holding it fixed during
imagination should remove drift that is pure error. If $C$ is something we fitted, holding it fixed
should damage the rollout. We test that by projecting the latent back onto the level set of $C$ at
every imagination step,
\begin{equation}
z \;\leftarrow\; z - \alpha\,\bigl(C(z) - C_0\bigr)\,\frac{\nabla C(z)}{\|\nabla C(z)\|^2},
\end{equation}
and mapping the correction back so the part of $h$ outside the extracted subspace stays untouched.
We retrain nothing. The model stays frozen and the edit happens at decoding time.

\paragraph{We score the slope over a fixed grid, not the best $\alpha$.} We sweep $\alpha$ over
$(0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4)$, identical for every arm, and report the whole curve. Taking the minimum
over a grid once told us an arm had improved by 5.9\% when its curve rose monotonically to twice its
baseline error. The direction of the curve is the result; the minimum is a selection over five
chances.

\paragraph{Three arms, and the random one is a distribution.} The first enforces each conservative
model's own recovered $C$. The second enforces a random polynomial over the same degree-4 basis,
matched in norm, on the same models. The third enforces each dissipative model's own recovered $C$.
The random arm is the one that can kill the result: if projection improves rollouts whatever we
enforce, then the edit shrinks the rollout toward a manifold and has nothing to do with physics.
That arm is therefore drawn twenty times per model rather than once, and scored as a null
distribution with the recovered law's percentile inside it. The same twenty polynomials run on
every model, so a difference between models is the model and not the draw.

Figure~\ref{fig:three}c shows the outcome. The recovered law reduces rollout error by 2.9\%, 3.3\%
and 3.5\% at the strongest projection, on all three seeds. Across the sixty random laws the median
is 53.9\% \emph{worse}, and only 7 of 60 improve the rollout at all. The same edit on the
dissipative models does nothing, moving error by 0.5\%, which is what should happen when the level
set we enforce is not a law.

\paragraph{The arms differ more in variance than in median.} The recovered law's per-seed slopes span
$0.016$. The random draws' slopes span $3.71$, which is $234\times$ wider. Enforcing the recovered law
does the same small good thing on every seed. Enforcing a random law does something different and
usually harmful each time.

\paragraph{The specificity is strong on two seeds and weak on the third.} Per model, the recovered
law's slope sits at the 0th percentile of that model's own twenty draws on two seeds, better than
every random law, and at the 15th percentile on the third (Figure~\ref{fig:null}). On that third
model 7 of 20 random laws improve the rollout, and the best of them reaches $-21.9\%$, seven times
the improvement the recovered law achieves there. So the claim the evidence supports is that the
effect is specific to the recovered law on two models of three, and that on the third a projection
onto almost any smooth level set helps more. We do not know what distinguishes that model.

\paragraph{The scoring rule is why we can say that.} The same seed's best random law is not monotone:
its error falls to $\alpha = 0.2$ and then rises. A single-$\alpha$ comparison would have reported a
random law beating the recovered one; the fixed-grid slope rule reports the curve instead. This is
the second time that rule changed a conclusion for us. It is also why the twenty-draw null was worth
its cost: the earlier version of this experiment drew one polynomial and reused it on all three
models, which made a single lucky or unlucky draw look like a three-seed result.

\paragraph{Takeaway.} The recovered quantity is causally load-bearing for the model's own rollouts,
and the effect is specific to it. The magnitude is small. The GRU study measured 68\% for what is
procedurally the same edit, but the two numbers are not comparable: it scored error in the
oscillator's state space, while we score pixel error against held-out video, where most of each
frame is static background that reconstructs correctly whatever the latent does. Part of the gap is
therefore definitional. What remains could come from the architecture, the system, or the extraction
dimension, and we have not separated those.
