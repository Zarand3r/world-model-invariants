\section{The correction acts on causally important directions}
\label{sec:leverage}

Section~\ref{sec:intervention} shows that enforcing the recovered scalar can improve rollout
accuracy. We next ask which latent directions the intervention changes.

For each direction $u$ in the extracted subspace, we measure its variance and the damage caused by
perturbing the latent state along it:
\begin{equation}
V(u) = \operatorname{Var}(u^\top z), \qquad
D_H(u) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[L_H(z+\epsilon u) - L_H(z)\bigr],
\label{eq:leverage}
\end{equation}
where $L_H$ is the model's $H$-step imagination error against held-out video. $V$ is the quantity PCA
uses to rank directions. $D_H$ measures causal sensitivity of the rollout and does not require
simulator state. We use $\epsilon = 0.25|z|$, based on the calibration in Appendix~\ref{sec:repro}.

A perturbation injected into the latent state persists during imagination rather than decaying away.
After 40 steps, the perturbation has grown to $6.3$ times its initial size for $\epsilon = 0.05|z|$,
$2.8$ times for $0.25|z|$, and $1.9$ times for $0.5|z|$, taking the median across the three models.
Larger perturbations amplify less. Small latent errors can therefore produce substantial rollout
differences over the horizons we study.

\paragraph{Variance and causal importance disagree at short horizons.} At a 40-step horizon,
variance rank and damage rank are anticorrelated on all three models: $-0.364$, $-0.259$, and
$-0.406$. The damage caused by the most and least consequential directions differs by
$13.5$--$118.8$ times across models (Figure~\ref{fig:leverage}a). The damage ranking itself is
reproducible: splitting the evaluation trajectories in half gives a median rank correlation of
$0.95$ across the settings we tested, with a minimum of $0.52$ at the smallest perturbation and
longest horizon.

This disagreement weakens at longer horizons. Pooling three models and three perturbation sizes, the
median $\rho(V, D_H)$ is $-0.252$ at horizon 20 and is negative in all 9 settings; $-0.196$ at
horizon 50 and negative in 5 of 9 settings; and $-0.028$ at horizon 100 and negative in 5 of 9
settings, with one setting reaching $+0.727$. Once the rollout has already diverged substantially,
high-amplitude directions can dominate total error and the rankings begin to realign.

We therefore make a limited claim: at short horizons, PCA variance can rank latent directions
differently from their causal effect on prediction. We measure this only on the conservative models.

\paragraph{The invariant correction tends to push along high-damage directions.} We also measure how
strongly the projection in Equation~\ref{eq:edit} pushes along each latent direction. That quantity
correlates with $D_H$ at $+0.385$, $+0.692$, and $+0.287$ across the three models
(Figure~\ref{fig:leverage}b). The projection was never given the damage ranking. The correlations
remain well below $1$, so the correction is only partly concentrated on the most consequential
directions.

\subsection{Basis-independent properties reproduce better than coordinate-level selectors}
\label{sec:charts}

The three independently trained models agree closely on several quantities that do not depend on a
particular latent basis. Their energy-recovery scores vary by only $0.008$, their intervention slopes
by $0.016$, and the top-3 eigenvalue mass of
\begin{equation*}
M_C = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{gg^\top}{\|g\|^2}\right], \qquad g = \nabla C,
\end{equation*}
by $0.046$, with values $0.448$, $0.441$, and $0.487$.

Selectors defined on individual latent coordinates are much less stable. Ranking coordinates by
$\nabla C$ and keeping the top three gives intervention slopes of $-0.075$, $+0.016$, and $-0.089$:
this beats a variance-based ranking on two models, loses on the third, and changes sign across seeds.
Replacing the coordinate mask with an orthogonal projector onto the top eigenspace of $M_C$ beats the
variance selector on all three models. On one model, however, it falls only at the 41st percentile of
100 random rank-3 subspaces. It is therefore better than variance on that model without being
distinguishable from a random subspace of the same rank.

The spectrum of $M_C$ also shows that three of the twelve directions contain just under half of its
total mass. Because the correction has rank one at each state, this spectrum measures how much
$\nabla C$ rotates as the state changes. The amount of rotation is similar across all three models.

These measurements are consistent with the three models representing the same scalar invariant in
different latent coordinate systems. We treat that as an interpretation, not a direct measurement.
Raw latent bases are arbitrary across independently trained models, so comparing coordinate-aligned
subspaces would not be meaningful without an explicit alignment. What reproduces here are
basis-independent properties of the scalar, not a fixed set of latent coordinates.
